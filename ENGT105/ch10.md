# CHAPTER 10: "The Diamond as Big as the Ritz" — F. Scott Fitzgerald

## 1. Chapter Overview

**What it's about:** A satirical, fantastical short story about John T. Unger, a boy from a small town called Hades who visits the secret Montana estate of his wealthy schoolmate Percy Washington. The Washington family's fortune rests on owning a literal mountain made of a single, enormous diamond. To protect this secret, the family lives in complete isolation, employs enslaved workers who don't know slavery has been abolished, murders any outside guests who might reveal the secret, and ultimately faces destruction when captive aviators (former guests who escaped) return with an air attack. The story ends with the family's death/destruction and John's escape with the Washington daughters, Kismine and Jasmine — now penniless.

**Central idea:** This is a **satire of American wealth, greed, and the corrupting, isolating logic of unlimited capitalism** — Fitzgerald pushes the "American Dream" premise of limitless wealth to an absurd, monstrous extreme, showing that infinite riches produce not utopia but moral horror, isolation, paranoia, and ultimately self-destruction.

**Why it matters:** This is the longest and most narratively complex text on your syllabus — expect detailed plot/character questions, a heavy focus on satire and symbolism, and likely essay questions about the story's critique of wealth and the American Dream.

**Detailed summary in order (by section):**

1. **Section I:** Introduces John T. Unger, from the small town of Hades, sent to the elite St. Midas' School near Boston. He befriends Percy Washington, a quiet, wealthy, secretive classmate. Percy invites John to spend the summer at his home "in the West," and on the train reveals: "My father is by far the richest man in the world" — and that his father owns "a diamond bigger than the Ritz-Carlton Hotel."
2. **Section II:** They arrive at the tiny, desolate village of Fish, Montana (population twelve "somber and inexplicable" men who exist only to watch the train pass). A hidden, opulent car (driven by enslaved Black servants) takes them the rest of the way to the Washington estate, which is completely hidden in a remote mountain valley.
3. **Section III:** John wakes in the luxurious château and is bathed/groomed by a servant (Gygsum) in an elaborate, almost surreal bathing ritual (a bath stocked with live fish, rose-scented soap, music piped in).
4. **Section IV:** Percy tells John the family's origin story: his ancestor **Fitz-Norman Culpepper Washington**, a former Confederate colonel, moved West after the Civil War with a group of enslaved Black people who worshipped him and did not know slavery had ended. Fitz-Norman accidentally discovered that an entire mountain was solid diamond (via a squirrel that dropped a shiny object). Realizing the diamond's value was so enormous it would collapse the world economy if revealed, he secretly sold small quantities of diamonds abroad (to the Russian Czar, etc.) to accumulate a fortune in gold, then **sealed up the mine** to protect the secret, ensuring the family's descendants would live in permanent, secret luxury.
5. **Section V:** John explores the estate's grounds and meets **Kismine**, Percy's younger sister, age 16 (John's age), described as strikingly beautiful, seemingly innocent and naive. They fall into an immediate, flirtatious connection.
6. **Sections VI–VII:** Over the following weeks/months, John and Kismine fall in love and secretly plan to marry (eventually deciding to elope, since her father would never approve a marriage to an outsider).
7. **Section VIII:** John learns disturbing details about the estate: Braddock Washington (Percy's father, current family patriarch) had kidnapped various professionals (a landscape gardener, architect, stage designer, decadent poet) to design the estate, but when they couldn't agree/perform, he simply had them declared insane and committed to an asylum. More importantly, in this section the story's darkest twist is revealed: Kismine admits, through tears, that the family **has had other guests before** — and that her father **has all of them murdered** (drugged in their sleep) at the end of each summer, specifically so they can never leave and reveal the family's secret. Kismine's older sister Jasmine keeps inviting new friends anyway, seemingly indifferent to (or in some kind of denial about) their fate. Kismine tearfully admits she now realizes John will be killed too — and that she's been aware of this the whole time she's been falling in love with him. John is horrified; the two argue, but eventually reconcile, and Kismine insists on eloping with him immediately rather than let him be killed. Braddock Washington briefly interrupts and catches them together, increasing their urgency to escape.
8. **Section IX:** As John and Kismine prepare to escape (with Jasmine joining them), the estate comes under sudden military attack: a squadron of **airplanes** appears, revealed to belong to former captive guests (aviators) who had previously managed to escape and are now returning with an armed assault to destroy the Washington estate and free/avenge past victims. A prolonged, devastating aerial battle ensues.
9. **Section X (the climax):** As dawn breaks and the family's anti-aircraft defenses are destroyed, John secretly witnesses Braddock Washington performing an extraordinary act: standing on the mountain with two enslaved men holding an enormous diamond, Washington delivers a **desperate, grandiose speech attempting to bribe God** — offering to build the most magnificent diamond cathedral/monument imaginable (with a horrifying detail: an altar where he'd sacrifice "even the greatest and most powerful man alive" for God's amusement) — in exchange for erasing the last day's events and restoring things to how they were before the attack. **God does not respond/accept the bribe** (nature briefly darkens, then resumes normally — signaling refusal). Washington and his wife, son, and the two enslaved men then descend into a secret trap-door in the mountain, and immediately after, the entire mountain and château **explode/detonate** — apparently a defensive self-destruct mechanism Washington had built as an ultimate contingency, designed to destroy everything (including himself and his family) rather than let the diamond's existence be discovered or exploited by outsiders.
10. **Section XI (the ending):** John, Kismine, and Jasmine, now the only survivors, sit down to eat sandwiches from a picnic basket at a safe distance from the destroyed estate. John asks Kismine to show him what diamonds she managed to grab while escaping — but she reveals, with a laugh, that she grabbed the WRONG drawer and actually has only worthless **rhinestones**, not real diamonds. The family's entire fortune is gone; John, Kismine, and Jasmine are now completely penniless. The story ends on a note of philosophical resignation: John reflects that youth itself is "a form of chemical madness," and the story closes with the characters settling in for the night under the stars, materially ruined but alive.

## 2. Characters / Important Figures

- **John T. Unger** — the protagonist, from Hades (a small, ordinary Mississippi River town). **Personality/traits:** wide-eyed, impressionable, values wealth and status (raised in a culture that "worships" riches) but retains a basic moral compass that is genuinely horrified by the murders. **Motivations:** initially curiosity/social ambition (wanting to see genuine, extraordinary wealth); later, romantic love for Kismine and simple self-preservation once he learns the truth. **Development:** moves from naive admiration of wealth to horrified disillusionment, and ultimately to hard-won, humbled acceptance of loss (ending the story poor but alive, and philosophically resigned). **Why he matters:** functions as the reader's surrogate — an "ordinary" (if privileged-adjacent) outsider through whose eyes we witness and judge the Washington family's monstrous excess.

- **Percy Washington** — John's schoolmate, Braddock Washington's son. **Traits:** secretive, quietly arrogant, entirely normalized to his family's grotesque wealth and morality (he doesn't seem troubled by the murders — he's grown up considering it natural). **Role:** the initial "gateway" character who draws John into the Washington world; less developed emotionally than Kismine, mostly a plot device to introduce John to the estate.

- **Kismine Washington** — Percy's younger sister, John's love interest. **Traits:** beautiful, initially seems innocent/naive ("I never smoke, or drink... I think sophisticated is the last thing you can say about me") but this innocence is revealed to be a kind of moral blindness/normalization to her family's horrifying practices, not genuine goodness — she admits she "grew quite attached" to some of the murdered guests but never questioned the practice until she fell in love with John specifically. **Motivations:** genuine (if late-developing) love for John; also a somewhat childlike, morally undeveloped worldview shaped entirely by extreme privilege. **Development:** her arc is the story's central moral test — she moves from casual acceptance of her family's murders to active rebellion (choosing to elope with John rather than let him be killed) — though the story's final ironic joke (grabbing rhinestones instead of diamonds) suggests she remains, at heart, somewhat comically impractical/detached from real-world consequences even after this moral awakening. **Why she matters:** represents the moral corruption/blindness that extreme wealth and isolation can produce even in someone who isn't depicted as evil, just morally unformed by her sheltered, monstrous upbringing.

- **Jasmine Washington** — Kismine's older sister. **Traits:** in contrast to Kismine, depicted as more "ordinary"/middle-class in temperament despite her wealth (loves practical things like doing laundry, reads sentimental novels about poor girls). **Significant detail:** SHE is the one who keeps inviting new guests/friends to the estate, apparently in some form of denial or indifference about their fate — a darker, more disturbing detail than Kismine's passive acceptance. **Role:** provides a contrast to Kismine and a final, gently comic note at the story's end (her contentment with simple domestic tasks like doing laundry, even in poverty).

- **Braddock Washington** — Percy and Kismine's father, the current patriarch, richest man in the world. **Traits:** utterly authoritarian, uninterested in others' opinions, paranoid about protecting his secret, capable of extreme cruelty (murdering guests, having architects/designers committed to asylums) without any apparent moral hesitation, ultimately revealed to have an almost megalomaniacal relationship to power itself — his climactic attempt to literally **bribe God** shows he sees no limit to what money/grandeur should be able to purchase, even divine intervention. **Development:** his arc culminates in the bribery scene and his choice to detonate/destroy everything (himself, his family, the estate) rather than surrender or be discovered — an act of ultimate, self-annihilating pride/control. **Why he matters:** the story's central symbol of unchecked wealth's logical endpoint — total moral corruption, paranoid isolation, and finally, self-destruction rather than loss of control.

- **Fitz-Norman Culpepper Washington** — Braddock's ancestor, founder of the family fortune. **Role:** his origin story (Section IV) establishes the family's foundational sins: the discovery of the diamond mountain, the deliberate deception of enslaved workers to keep them ignorant that slavery had ended, and the decision to seal the mine and build a life of secret, isolated luxury rather than share or reveal the discovery. **Significance:** his choices set the template for everything that follows — the family's entire existence is built on deception, exploitation, and paranoid secrecy from the very beginning.

- **Mrs. Washington** — Braddock's wife, described as aloof, reserved, and almost entirely focused on her son Percy; a minor character mostly illustrating the family's general emotional coldness and dysfunction.

- **The enslaved Black servants** — kept in bondage and deliberately deceived (via a fabricated "proclamation" that the South won the Civil War) into believing slavery still exists and was never abolished. **Significance:** this is one of the story's darkest and most pointed satirical elements — the ultimate privilege of extreme wealth, in Fitzgerald's telling, is the power to literally rewrite reality/history for those under your control; a stark, damning image of wealth's power to exploit and deceive.

- **The captive aviators (background/plot device)** — former guests who had somehow escaped before being murdered; they return with an armed squadron of airplanes to attack and destroy the estate. **Significance:** function as a form of "return of the repressed" — the consequences of the family's crimes finally catching up with them, delivering the story's climactic destruction.

## 3. Themes

**Theme 1: The corrupting and isolating nature of unlimited wealth**
- How it appears: the family's complete physical isolation (a hidden valley, a village of only twelve men nearby who don't even know what's there); their casual, systematic murder of anyone who might expose their secret; Braddock Washington's absolute, unquestioned authority.
- What Fitzgerald is saying: wealth without limit doesn't produce paradise or freedom — it produces paranoia, moral corruption, and total isolation from the rest of humanity; the family's isolation is not just physical but moral — they have become entirely disconnected from ordinary human ethics.

**Theme 2: Satire of the American Dream and the myth of limitless prosperity**
- How it appears: the story takes the "self-made man" myth (Fitz-Norman's rags-to-riches discovery) and follows its logic to an absurd, monstrous extreme — the family becomes "the richest" not through virtue or hard work but through hoarding, deception, and murder.
- What Fitzgerald is saying: unrestrained pursuit of wealth, taken to its logical extreme, reveals itself as fundamentally incompatible with morality, community, and even (ultimately) survival — this is widely read as a critique of the Gilded Age/Jazz Age obsession with wealth that Fitzgerald explored throughout his career (most famously in *The Great Gatsby*, written around the same period).

**Theme 3: Slavery, exploitation, and the deliberate deception of the powerless**
- How it appears: the Washington family keeps its Black servants enslaved by literally lying to them about the outcome of the Civil War.
- What Fitzgerald is saying: this is one of the story's most pointed satirical elements — showing how extreme wealth/power doesn't just exploit people materially but can control their very access to truth and reality; a scathing image of the mechanisms of oppression.

**Theme 4: The destructiveness of secrecy and the impossibility of sustaining an isolated "perfect" world**
- How it appears: the family's entire existence depends on total secrecy (murdering guests, sealing the mine, hiding from the outside world); this secrecy ultimately fails and destroys them (the returning aviators).
- What Fitzgerald is saying: attempts to build a walled-off, secret utopia — cut off from consequence and community — are inherently unstable and doomed to collapse, often violently.

**Theme 5: Pride, hubris, and the illusion of total control (Braddock's bribe to God)**
- How it appears: Braddock Washington's climactic attempt to bribe God with an even greater diamond monument, offering human sacrifice as part of the deal.
- What Fitzgerald is saying: this is the story's most explicit statement of hubris — a man who has never encountered a limit to what money can buy assumes even DIVINE forces are negotiable; God's silent refusal (and the subsequent destruction) represents the ultimate, cosmic limit that no amount of wealth can overcome.

**Theme 6: The loss of innocence and the ambiguous value of disillusionment**
- How it appears: John's arc from wide-eyed admiration of wealth to horrified knowledge of its true cost; the ending's ironic reveal (rhinestones, not diamonds) strips away material wealth entirely, leaving the characters with only each other.
- What Fitzgerald is saying: the loss of illusions (about wealth, about love, about the family's glamour) is presented ambiguously — painful, but perhaps also a kind of liberation; the story's closing meditation on youth as "a form of chemical madness" suggests disillusionment is simply an inevitable part of growing up/maturing.

## 4. Literary Concepts & Techniques

- **Satire (the story's defining mode)** — Fitzgerald exaggerates the premise of extreme wealth to an absurd degree (a literal mountain of diamond; murdering all guests; bribing God) specifically to expose and critique real cultural attitudes toward wealth and status. *Why used:* satire allows Fitzgerald to make a serious social critique (about American materialism, the corrupting nature of wealth, the myth of the self-made man) through exaggeration and dark comedy rather than direct, didactic argument.
- **Symbolism — the diamond/diamond mountain** — represents wealth itself, and specifically the idea of wealth as something that, taken to its absolute extreme, becomes monstrous, isolating, and ultimately self-destructive rather than a source of genuine happiness or security. *Why it matters:* the diamond is literally the mountain the family lives on/in — wealth isn't just what they HAVE, it's the very ground they stand on and the foundation of their entire existence, making its eventual destruction total and final.
- **Symbolism — Hades (John's hometown)** — an ironic, obvious name (Hades = the Greek underworld) for what is actually an ordinary, modest American town; contrasted with the Washington estate, which initially appears heavenly/utopian but is revealed to be the TRUE hell (murder, slavery, moral corruption). *Why it matters:* this inversion (the town literally named after the underworld turns out to be safer and more moral than the beautiful, luxurious estate) is a key ironic structural device — Fitzgerald is subverting expectations about where true "hell" actually lies.
- **Symbolism — rhinestones vs. diamonds (the ending)** — the final ironic twist, where Kismine's grabbed "jewels" turn out to be worthless fakes. *Why it matters:* symbolically strips the story's central obsession (real, immense wealth) away entirely at the very end, leaving only the human relationships (John, Kismine, Jasmine) that remain — suggesting these relationships, not material wealth, are what actually endures/matters.
- **Irony (multiple layers)** — dramatic irony (readers sense something is deeply wrong with the estate's perfection well before John fully realizes it); situational irony (the "hellish"-named hometown is safer than the beautiful estate; the ending's rhinestones instead of diamonds; Braddock's failed bribe to God). *Why used:* irony reinforces the satirical critique throughout — expectations (about wealth, safety, beauty, power) are repeatedly inverted.
- **Hyperbole/exaggeration** — the sheer scale of the Washington fortune (a literal mountain of diamond, "the richest man in the world," an underground château with impossibly elaborate luxuries like a fish-filled sunken bath). *Why used:* core to the satirical mode — pushing wealth to a literally impossible, absurd extreme forces readers to see and critically examine real cultural attitudes toward wealth that might otherwise go unquestioned.
- **Gothic/dark fantasy elements** — the hidden valley, the secret trap-door, the self-destruct mechanism, the bribe to God, the aerial battle — give the story an almost mythic, fable-like quality despite its 20th-century American setting. *Why used:* elevates the satire beyond simple realism into something closer to a modern parable or cautionary tale.
- **Foreshadowing** — early hints of something being "off" (Percy's secrecy about his family, the ominous village of Fish, the elaborate security/isolation of the estate) build tension well before the murder revelation and the climactic battle. *Why used:* creates suspense and rewards attentive reading; also mirrors John's own gradual disillusionment.
- **Tone:** Begins whimsical, almost fairy-tale-like (the opulent description of the estate, the "dream"-like luxury); shifts to horror once the murders are revealed; becomes darkly comic/absurd at the climax (the bribe to God); ends on a note of wry, philosophical resignation.

## 5. Important Passages / Ideas

- **"My father is by far the richest man in the world" / "My father has a diamond bigger than the Ritz-Carlton Hotel."** — the story's inciting revelation; establishes the premise and Percy's casual, almost boastful normalization of unimaginable wealth.
- **Fitz-Norman's origin story (Section IV)** — the discovery of the diamond mountain, the deliberate deception of enslaved workers about the Civil War's outcome, and the decision to seal the mine. CRITICAL passage — establishes the family's foundational moral corruption; likely source of a "how did the Washington fortune originate, and what does this reveal" question.
- **Kismine's confession about the murdered guests (Section VIII)** — THE central plot/thematic turning point of the story; almost certainly the subject of a major exam question. Key details: guests are drugged and killed, usually in August/September; families are told they died of scarlet fever; Jasmine keeps inviting new guests despite this; Kismine admits she knew John would be killed too but fell in love with him anyway.
- **Braddock Washington's attempted bribe to God (Section X)** — the story's philosophical/thematic climax; likely source of a major analytical or essay question about hubris, the limits of wealth, and the story's use of religious/cosmic imagery.
- **The ending's rhinestone reveal** — key ironic final twist; likely source of a question about the story's conclusion and its thematic significance (the stripping away of material wealth, what remains when it's gone).
- **John's closing reflection: "It was a dream... Everybody's youth is a dream, a form of chemical madness."** — key closing philosophical statement; likely source of a "what is the significance of the story's ending/final lines" question.

## 6. Deeper Interpretation

- Historical/authorial context: Fitzgerald wrote this story in 1922, during the early "Jazz Age" — the same period and thematic territory he would explore more famously in *The Great Gatsby* (1925). Fitzgerald was famously preoccupied throughout his career with the American obsession with wealth, class, and status, and with the moral costs and illusions this obsession produces — "The Diamond as Big as the Ritz" is essentially a more fantastical, satirical dry run for themes he'd later treat with more psychological realism in Gatsby.
- Underlying tension: **the American Dream's promise of limitless opportunity/wealth vs. the moral and human costs of actually achieving limitless wealth.** The story doesn't just criticize excessive greed in the abstract — it specifically ties the Washington fortune's origin to the legacy of slavery and the post-Civil War South, suggesting that some of America's greatest fortunes are built on foundational, unresolved historical sins that require ongoing deception and violence to maintain.
- Subtle detail examiners may expect: notice that John himself is not a purely innocent outside critic — he comes from a town (Hades) that, per the story's opening, worships wealth as "the first article of its creed," and his own reason for staying and getting emotionally invested is initially just fascination with Percy's wealth. The story doesn't let readers feel entirely superior/separate from the Washingtons' values — John's own culture already primed him to admire exactly this kind of excess, even though he's horrified once he sees its true cost. This complicates a purely simple "good outsider vs. evil wealthy family" reading.
- Possible interpretations: (1) a straightforward satire of American greed/materialism; (2) a specifically historical critique of wealth built on the unaddressed legacy of slavery in the American South; (3) a more existential/philosophical fable about the impossibility of total control, secrecy, and isolation (Braddock's failed bribe to God, the inevitable collapse of the hidden utopia); (4) a coming-of-age story about the necessary, if painful, loss of illusions (John's arc, the final "chemical madness" reflection).

## 7. Exam Questions (with key points)

**Short answer:**
- Q: How did the Washington family fortune originate?
  - Key points: Fitz-Norman Culpepper Washington discovered an entire mountain of solid diamond in Montana after the Civil War; secretly sold small quantities abroad to accumulate gold; sealed the mine to protect the secret from causing global economic panic.

- Q: What happens to guests who visit the Washington estate?
  - Key points: they are eventually murdered (drugged in their sleep, usually by late summer) so they can never reveal the family's secret; their families are falsely told they died of scarlet fever.

**Explanation/analysis:**
- Q: Explain the significance of Braddock Washington's attempted bribe to God near the story's climax.
  - Key points: offers to build an unimaginably grand diamond monument (including a horrifying human-sacrifice altar) in exchange for erasing the day's events; represents the ultimate expression of his belief that wealth/power has no limits; God's silent refusal represents the one force his fortune cannot control or purchase; directly precedes the family's total self-destruction.

- Q: How does the deception of the enslaved workers function within the story's broader themes?
  - Key points: Fitz-Norman fabricates a "proclamation" claiming the South won the Civil War, deliberately keeping his Black workers enslaved and ignorant of abolition; ties the family's wealth directly to the unresolved legacy of slavery; shows wealth/power's capacity to control not just people's labor but their access to truth/reality itself.

**Long/essay:**
- Q: Discuss "The Diamond as Big as the Ritz" as a satire of the American Dream and American attitudes toward wealth.
  - Key points: define satire and its function; trace the exaggerated premise (literal mountain of diamond, richest man in the world); discuss the moral costs revealed (murder, slavery, paranoia, isolation); discuss the story's ending (total material collapse, rhinestones instead of diamonds) as the satire's final punchline/judgment on the pursuit of limitless wealth.

- Q: Analyze the character of Kismine Washington. How does her arc reflect the story's themes about wealth and moral corruption?
  - Key points: initial performed innocence/naivety vs. underlying moral blindness (casual acceptance of guest murders); her love for John as the catalyst for genuine moral awakening/rebellion; the final rhinestone irony as a comic but meaningful detail suggesting she remains somewhat detached from practical reality even after this awakening; her arc humanizes the story's critique — she's not depicted as simply evil, but as a product of monstrous, isolating privilege.

- Q: What is the significance of the story's ending, in which the family's fortune is revealed to be lost (rhinestones instead of diamonds)?
  - Key points: symbolically strips away the story's central subject (wealth) entirely; leaves only human relationships/survival; connects to John's closing "chemical madness" reflection on youth and disillusionment; can be read as either a bleak or a strangely liberating ending — worth discussing both possibilities.

**Character question:**
- Q: Compare John T. Unger at the beginning and end of the story. How has he changed?
  - Key points: begins as an impressionable, wealth-admiring boy from a culture that reveres riches; ends as someone who has witnessed the true moral cost of unlimited wealth (murder, slavery, his own near-death), lost the girl's fortune along with his own prospects, and arrived at a resigned, philosophical acceptance of loss and disillusionment as part of "youth."

**Literary-device question:**
- Q: How does Fitzgerald use irony throughout the story to support its satirical purpose?
  - Key points: Hades (hometown) is safer/more moral than the beautiful estate; the ending's rhinestones instead of diamonds; Braddock's failed bribe to an unresponsive God; discuss how each instance of irony undercuts and critiques the value system (wealth = safety/happiness/power) the story is satirizing.

**Compare/contrast:**
- Q: Compare the theme of hubris/overreaching pride in "The Diamond as Big as the Ritz" and "The Myth of Sisyphus."
  - Key points: Braddock Washington's hubris (bribing God, believing wealth has no limits) is punished/destroyed — a fairly traditional cautionary structure; Sisyphus's "defiance" of the gods (scorning death, loving life) is instead reframed by Camus as a source of dignity and even happiness, NOT punished narratively in the same way (the myth's punishment already happened; Camus asks us to see PAST it) — a good essay can contrast Fitzgerald's more traditional moral-consequence structure with Camus's radical reframing of defiance as meaningful/positive rather than simply punishable pride.
