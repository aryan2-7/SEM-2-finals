# What is Rhetoric?
Rhetoric is the way in which you communicate in everyday life.
These communications can be persuasive in nature and can be made of text, images, video, or any other type of media.
Rhetoric is the art of using language effectively and persuasively in spoken or written form.
It’s about choosing words and structuring ideas in a way that influences, informs, or entertains an audience.

<!-- Slide number: 3 -->
# Simple definition
Rhetoric is how we use language to achieve a purpose—especially to persuade.
Imagine you're trying to convince your friend to watch a movie with you. You might say:
"You have to watch this movie—it's so good! The story is gripping, and the acting is phenomenal. Everyone’s talking about it!“

That’s rhetoric—you’re using emotional appeal, exaggeration, and popular opinion to persuade.

<!-- Slide number: 4 -->
# Example
From Martin Luther King Jr.’s "I Have a Dream" speech:
"I have a dream that my four little children will one day live in a nation where they will not be judged by the color of their skin but by the content of their character.“
Here, King uses rhetoric to inspire and persuade his audience. He uses emotional appeal (pathos), repetition ("I have a dream"), and strong moral reasoning (ethos and logos).

# Rhetorical strategies
Rhetorical strategies are techniques that writers and speakers use to persuade, inform, or entertain their audience.
These strategies help shape how a message is delivered so it can have the desired effect on the audience.
Rhetorical strategies (also called rhetorical devices or appeals) involve the use of language and techniques to convince or influence an audience.
They are commonly found in speeches, essays, advertisements, and even everyday conversations.

# Three Core Rhetorical Appeals (Aristotle's Rhetorical Triangle):
Ethos (Appeal to Credibility)
Convincing the audience that the speaker is trustworthy or an authority on the subject.
Example:
“As a doctor with over 20 years of experience, I can assure you that this treatment is safe.”
The speaker is establishing their expertise, making the audience more likely to trust them.

<!-- Slide number: 9 -->
# Pathos
Persuading by appealing to the audience’s emotions.
Example:
“If we don’t act now, thousands of children will go to bed hungry tonight.”
This creates a sense of urgency and empathy, encouraging people to take action.

<!-- Slide number: 10 -->
# Logos
Using facts, statistics, or logical arguments to persuade.
Example:
“Studies show that students who use digital tools in class score 20% higher on tests.”
This relies on evidence and reason rather than feelings or trust.
