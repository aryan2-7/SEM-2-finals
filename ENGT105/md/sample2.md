## Q2. Svetlovidov's Social Status in Youth vs. His Pain in Old Age

**What is the correlation between Vasili Svetlovidov's social status in his youth and the subsequent pain he undergoes in his old age? Explain.**

### Model Answer

Svetlovidov's pain in old age is directly rooted in the peculiar social status he held as a young, celebrated actor — a status that was simultaneously elevated (publicly adored, applauded) and precarious (never fully respected as a person). This contradiction is the source of his later suffering.

In his youth, Svetlovidov enjoyed public adoration as a performer, but he reveals through his account of the woman he loved that this adoration was never matched by genuine social acceptance. She would only marry him if he gave up the stage, and he realizes that the audience who cheered him would never "demean themselves" by letting their daughters marry an actor. This moment is the play's turning point: Svetlovidov's "eyes were opened" to the fact that his social status was fundamentally hollow — he was a "plaything of their idle fancy," publicly celebrated yet privately looked down upon, never granted the stable social respectability of an ordinary profession or family life.

The correlation to his old age is direct and causal. Because his youthful status offered fame without belonging, he never built the family, home, or lasting relationships that might have sustained him in old age. Instead, this early realization caused him to "lose his sense of aim and direction" and "bury his talent" in minor, cheapened comic roles rather than serious ones — squandering the very gift that had defined his social status. By the time he is 68, waking alone and drunk in an empty, dark theatre, the consequences are total: no wife, no children, no family, profound loneliness ("I'm as lonely as the wind in the fields"). His youthful status — built on public performance rather than authentic connection — directly produced the isolation that defines his old age. The play thus suggests that a social status built purely on audience adoration, without genuine acceptance or belonging, is an unstable foundation that collapses painfully once the performances end and old age arrives.

### Why this works (exam notes)
- The question asks for a **correlation**, so the answer must explicitly trace cause-and-effect, not just describe youth and old age separately
- Directly cites the pivotal "woman who wanted him to give up the stage" backstory as the connecting hinge between the two time periods
- Ends by explicitly restating the causal claim, since "explain the correlation" questions are graded on whether the connection itself is made clear, not just on accurate details about each period

## Q4. Refuting One of Ramachandran's Ideas

**Assume you do not agree with one of Ramachandran's ideas (e.g., his critique of the "assembly-line approach"). Write your reasons why you refute this particular idea.**

### Model Answer (refuting his critique of the assembly-line/collaborative approach)

While V. S. Ramachandran's romanticized vision of Victorian-style, individual "tinkering" science is compelling, his dismissal of the modern "assembly-line" approach — papers with thirty authors — as something that "takes much of the joy out of the practice of science" is, I would argue, an incomplete and somewhat unfair critique.

First, Ramachandran's own examples of celebrated breakthroughs (Darwin, Faraday, Watson and Crick) come from earlier eras when scientific problems were, by modern standards, far more tractable to a single mind or small team. Contemporary challenges — genome sequencing, climate modeling, vaccine development, particle physics — are frequently so complex that they genuinely require the combined expertise of many specialists working in coordinated teams. Dismissing this collaborative structure as merely joyless "sciencemanship" risks undervaluing real, necessary scientific infrastructure that has produced enormous public benefit, such as the rapid multi-team development of COVID-19 vaccines.

Second, large collaborative science does not necessarily eliminate the "joy" or "passion" Ramachandran prizes — it can simply relocate it. A researcher on a thirty-author paper may still experience deep personal curiosity and satisfaction in their specific contribution, even if the paper's authorship list is long. Ramachandran's framing risks assuming that only lone-genius discovery counts as "real" scientific joy, which overlooks the genuine intellectual fulfillment many scientists find in specialized, collaborative work.

Finally, romanticizing the "Victorian" era of science somewhat overlooks that era's serious limitations — restricted access to scientific careers based on class, gender, and race, and the absence of the rigorous peer review and reproducibility standards that modern collaborative science, for all its bureaucratic frustrations, has helped establish. A fuller assessment would weigh what has been gained through modern scientific infrastructure, not only what Ramachandran feels has been lost.

### Why this works (exam notes)
- The question explicitly requires disagreement with a specific idea — this answer commits fully to refuting the "assembly-line" critique rather than hedging or agreeing with Ramachandran by the end
- Uses concrete counter-evidence (COVID-19 vaccine development) rather than purely abstract argument
- Engages directly with Ramachandran's own vocabulary ("sciencemanship," "Victorian") to show close reading, while still building an independent counter-argument

## Q5. Process Analysis vs. Process Description

**What makes process analysis different from process description? Explain with examples. You can also bring in references from a project you did this/last semester.**

### Model Answer

**Process description** explains HOW something is done or works — a straightforward, sequential account of steps, typically written so a reader can understand or replicate the process. It focuses on the "what happens" without necessarily evaluating why each step matters or what could go wrong.

**Process analysis**, by contrast, goes further: it examines WHY a process works the way it does, evaluates its effectiveness, identifies causes and effects at each stage, and often considers strengths, weaknesses, or alternatives. It is analytical and evaluative rather than purely descriptive.

**Example — Process Description:**
"To assemble the attendance-tracking circuit, first connect the RFID reader to the microcontroller's designated pins. Next, upload the reader-authentication code. Then, connect the display module and test for output." — This simply lists the steps in order.

**Example — Process Analysis:**
"In our SmartAttendance project this semester, we analyzed why our initial RFID-based check-in process caused delays during peak entry times. We found that sequential (rather than parallel) card-reading was the bottleneck: each card had to be fully processed before the next could begin. This analysis led us to implement a queued, asynchronous reading system, reducing average check-in time by 40%. Unlike a simple description of 'how the system reads cards,' this account explains WHY the original process was flawed and evaluates the reasoning behind our redesign."

The key distinction: description answers "what are the steps," while analysis answers "why do these steps work or fail, and what does that mean." A technical report often needs BOTH — a process description for a reader who needs to replicate the work, and a process analysis for a reader (like a supervisor or examiner) who needs to understand the reasoning and judge the quality of the approach.

### Why this works (exam notes)
- The question explicitly invites bringing in a real project reference — doing so (here, referencing a plausible SmartAttendance-style project) makes the answer concrete and personally grounded rather than purely theoretical
- The two examples are deliberately parallel (both about the same general topic — an attendance system) so the CONTRAST between description and analysis is easy to see side-by-side

## Q6. The Four Aspects of Document Design

**What are the four aspects of document design? What things are important in each of them?**

### Model Answer

Document design refers to the deliberate structuring and visual presentation of written content to improve clarity, usability, and reader comprehension. It consists of four key aspects:

**1. Organization**
The logical structuring of content into coherent sections. Important elements: clear headings and subheadings that reflect a sensible hierarchy; grouping related information together; ensuring the overall structure matches the document's purpose (e.g., problem → solution, or chronological order).

**2. Order**
The sequence in which information is presented to the reader. Important elements: presenting the most important or most relevant information first (especially in reports, following the "inverted pyramid" principle); maintaining a logical flow so each section builds naturally on the last; avoiding disorienting jumps between unrelated topics.

**3. Access**
The ease with which a reader can locate specific information within the document. Important elements: a table of contents and page numbers for longer documents; consistent use of headings/subheadings as navigational aids; effective use of white space to visually separate sections; indexing or cross-referencing where relevant.

**4. Variety**
The strategic use of visual variation to maintain reader engagement and highlight important content. Important elements: appropriate use of fonts, bold/italics for emphasis; incorporation of charts, tables, and diagrams where they clarify data better than prose; avoiding monotony without sacrificing consistency (variety should support clarity, not undermine it).

Together, these four aspects ensure that a technical document is not only accurate in content but also genuinely usable — a well-organized document with poor access (no headings, no white space) or a well-ordered document lacking variety (dense, undifferentiated text) will still frustrate readers, even if the underlying information is correct.

### Why this works (exam notes)
- This is the single most-recurring concept across your entire syllabus — expect to reproduce this almost verbatim on the real exam
- Each aspect gets its own clearly labeled sub-section with concrete, specific elements — not just a one-line definition
- The closing paragraph ties the four aspects together, showing you understand they work as an integrated system, not four disconnected checkboxes

## Q8. Memo — Embracing Online Classes Post-Pandemic

**Write a memo addressed to the Head of the Department of your school on "The need for embracing more of online classes in the post-pandemic times."**

### Model Answer

---

**MEMORANDUM**

**To:** Head of Department, Department of Computer Engineering
**From:** [Your Name], Student Representative
**Date:** [Date]
**Subject:** Proposal to Embrace Greater Use of Online Classes Post-Pandemic

I am writing to propose that the department consider expanding the use of online classes even as in-person instruction has fully resumed post-pandemic.

While the pandemic necessitated online learning out of urgency, our experience during that period revealed genuine, lasting advantages that remain relevant now. Online sessions allow students with long or difficult commutes to attend classes without losing productive time in transit. Recorded lectures also give students the flexibility to revisit complex technical material at their own pace — particularly valuable in subject-heavy courses like our engineering curriculum, where a single missed explanation can create lasting confusion.

Additionally, hybrid delivery would allow guest lecturers and industry professionals, who may not be able to travel to campus, to contribute to our coursework more easily, enriching our exposure to real-world engineering practice.

I recognize that in-person instruction offers irreplaceable benefits — hands-on lab work, spontaneous discussion, and stronger peer connection — and I am not proposing a full return to remote learning. Rather, I request that the department consider a hybrid model: retaining in-person instruction for lab-based and highly interactive courses, while offering select theory-heavy courses in a hybrid or fully online format, with recorded lecture access for all students.

I would welcome the opportunity to discuss this proposal further and would be glad to gather student feedback to support the department's decision-making process.

Sincerely,
[Your Name]

---

### Why this works (exam notes)
- Balanced, not one-sided — acknowledges genuine value of in-person learning before making the case for hybrid options, which is more persuasive than a purely one-sided argument
- Specific, concrete reasons (commute time, recorded lectures for review, guest lecturer access) rather than vague appeals to "flexibility"
- Ends with a collaborative, non-demanding closing tone appropriate for addressing a department head
