## Motional emf

The emf produced by the relative motion between the magnetic field and a conducting loop (or rod) is called motional emf.

### Flux rule for a rectangular loop moving in a uniform magnetic field

Suppose a portion $abcd$ of a conducting loop is inside a magnetic field $\vec{B}$, as shown in **Figure 1**. Let $\vec{v}$ be the velocity of the loop, $h$ the width $ab$, $s$ the length of the loop inside the field at time $t$, and $R$ the resistance connected with the loop.

When the loop moves, the charge particles inside $ab$ experience a Lorentz force $\vec{F}=q(\vec{v}\times\vec{B})$ due to the magnetic field. Therefore, the magnetic force experienced by a unit charge is

$$\vec{f}_{\text{mag}}=\vec{v}\times\vec{B}.$$

Along side $ab$, a unit charge experiences the magnetic force $(\vec{f}_{\text{mag}})_{ab}=vB$ along the direction from $a$ to $b$. The unit charge on side $ad$ or $bc$ experiences the magnetic force $(\vec{f}_{\text{mag}})_{ad}=(\vec{f}_{\text{mag}})_{bc}=vB$ along the direction normal to the side. The charge in the remaining portion does not experience the magnetic force because of the absence of the magnetic field.

The amount of work done by the magnetic force to drive a unit charge in a closed circuit is called the induced emf, i.e.,

$$\mathcal{E}=\oint \vec{f}_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\vec{l}=\int_a^b \vec{f}_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\vec{l}+\int_b^c\vec{f}_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\vec{l}+\int_c^d\vec{f}_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\vec{l}+\int_d^a\vec{f}_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\vec{l}$$

Along side $ab$, the force is parallel to $d\vec{l}$ ($\vec{f}_{\text{mag}}\parallel d\vec{l}$), so this term is non-vanishing. Along sides $bc$ and $da$ the force is perpendicular to $d\vec{l}$ ($\vec{f}_{\text{mag}}\perp d\vec{l}$), and along side $cd$ the magnetic field is zero ($\vec{B}=0$); hence these three terms vanish:

$$\therefore \mathcal{E}=vBh \tag{1}$$

Again, the magnetic flux crossing the whole circuital loop is

$$\Phi=\oint\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{A}=BA=Bsh$$

As the loop moves, the flux decreases, i.e.

$$\frac{d\Phi}{dt}=-B\frac{ds}{dt}h=-vBh$$

From equations (1) and (2),

$$\mathcal{E}=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \tag{3}$$

This is called the flux rule for motional emf.

### Flux rule for an arbitrary loop moving in a non-uniform magnetic field

Suppose a loop of arbitrary shape is moving with velocity $\vec{v}$ in a static magnetic field $\vec{B}$. The positions of the loop at times $t$ and $t+dt$ are shown in **Figure 2**. The change in flux due to the motion of the loop is then

$$d\Phi=\Phi(t+dt)-\Phi(t)=\Phi_{\text{ribbon}}=\oint_{\text{ribbon}}\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{a}$$

Here $d\vec{a}$ is the small area generated by $d\vec{l}$ and $\vec{v}\,dt$. So,

$$d\vec{a}=\vec{v}\,dt\times d\vec{l}=(\vec{v}\times d\vec{l})\,dt$$

$$\therefore d\Phi=\oint\vec{B}\cdot(\vec{v}\times d\vec{l})\,dt$$

$$\frac{d\Phi}{dt}=\oint\vec{B}\cdot(\vec{v}\times d\vec{l})=\oint(\vec{B}\times\vec{v})\cdot d\vec{l}=-\oint(\vec{v}\times\vec{B})\cdot d\vec{l} \tag{4}$$

The quantity $\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$ is the magnetic force per unit charge. Therefore,

$$\oint\vec{f}_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\vec{l}=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \tag{5}$$

The quantity on the right-hand side is called the induced emf. Therefore, from equations (4) and (5),

$$\mathcal{E}=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt}$$

## Faraday's Laws of Electromagnetic Induction

The phenomenon of generation of emf in a conducting loop due to the relative motion between a magnet and the loop is called electromagnetic induction, and the emf thus produced is called induced emf. Faraday's laws of electromagnetic induction are as follows:

(i) The change in magnetic flux produces an induced emf in the circuit,  
(ii) The induced emf lasts as long as the change in flux continues, and  
(iii) The induced emf is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux, i.e.

$$\mathcal{E}=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt}$$

Suppose a surface $S$ bounded by curve $C$ is in a time-varying magnetic field $\vec{B}$. Then the magnetic flux through the surface is given by

$$\Phi=\int_S\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{a}$$

Suppose $\vec{E}$ is the electric field produced by the induced emf; then

$$\mathcal{E}=\oint_C\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l},\qquad \therefore\ \oint_C\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt}$$

Applying Stokes' theorem on the left-hand side,

$$\int_S(\nabla\times\vec{E})\cdot d\vec{a}=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_S\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{a}=-\int_S\frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t}\cdot d\vec{a}$$

$$\therefore\ \nabla\times\vec{E}=-\frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t} \tag{6}$$

This is the differential form of Faraday's law. Also, $\vec{B}=\nabla\times\vec{A}$, so that

$$\nabla\times\vec{E}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\vec{A})=\nabla\times\left(-\frac{\partial\vec{A}}{\partial t}\right)$$

$$\therefore\ \vec{E}=-\frac{\partial\vec{A}}{\partial t} \tag{7}$$

This gives the electric field due to the induced emf.

## Lenz's Law

When a magnet is brought towards a coil, a current is induced in it due to the change in magnetic flux. The induced current itself produces a magnetic field and hence a magnetic flux. The direction of the induced current is given by Lenz's law. It states that the induced current is such that it opposes the change of flux which produced it. That means the flux due to the induced current always opposes the original flux which produced the current.

Lenz's law is in accordance with the law of conservation of energy, according to which energy is always conserved. Here, the induced emf is produced at the cost of mechanical energy.

From Faraday's law, $\nabla\times\vec{E}=-\partial\vec{B}/\partial t$, and from Ampere's law, $\nabla\times\vec{B}=\mu_0\vec{J}$. As $\vec{E}$ is purely a Faraday-induced electric field, $\nabla\cdot\vec{E}=0$, and also for the magnetic field, $\nabla\cdot\vec{B}=0$.

Thus, the Faraday-induced electric field can be determined from $-\partial\vec{B}/\partial t$ exactly in the same way as the magnetic field is determined from $\mu_0\vec{J}$. Thus, from the Biot-Savart law, we have

$$\vec{B}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec{J}\times\vec{r}}{r^3}\,d\tau$$

The analog to the Biot-Savart law is

$$\vec{E}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{(\partial\vec{B}/\partial t)\times\vec{r}}{r^3}\,d\tau$$

$$\therefore\ \vec{E}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{d}{dt}\int\frac{\vec{B}\times\vec{r}}{r^3}\,d\tau \tag{8}$$

Again we know that

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{A}=0,\quad \nabla\times\vec{A}=\vec{B}$$

and

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{B}=0,\quad \nabla\times\vec{B}=\mu_0\vec{J}$$

So $\vec{A}$ depends on $\vec{B}$ exactly in the same way as $\vec{B}$ depends on $\mu_0\vec{J}$. Since

$$\vec{B}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec{J}\times\vec{r}}{r^3}\,d\tau,$$

it must be the case that

$$\vec{A}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec{B}\times\vec{r}}{r^3}\,d\tau$$

Thus, equation (8) can be written as

$$\vec{E}=-\frac{\partial\vec{A}}{\partial t}$$

This is the expression for the electric field due to the Faraday-induced emf. The result can be checked by applying the curl, i.e.

$$\nabla\times\vec{E}=-\nabla\times\frac{\partial\vec{A}}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\vec{A})=-\frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t}$$

## Self Induction

Suppose a current $I$ is flowing through a coil, thereby producing a magnetic field. If the current through the coil changes, the magnetic flux linked with the coil also changes, and this changing flux produces an emf called induced emf or back emf in the coil. The direction of the current due to the induced emf is opposite to the direction of the original current. The phenomenon by which an oppositely induced emf is produced is called self induction.

It is found that the magnetic flux linked with the coil is proportional to the current flowing through it. Let $\Phi$ denote the flux; then

$$\Phi\propto I \Rightarrow \Phi=LI$$

Here $L$ is a constant called the coefficient of self induction of the coil or self inductance. Also, from Faraday's law, the induced emf $\mathcal{E}$ is

$$\mathcal{E}=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt}=-L\frac{dI}{dt}$$

$$\therefore\ L=\frac{\mathcal{E}}{(-dI/dt)}$$

Thus, the coefficient of self induction (self inductance) is defined as the ratio of the induced emf to the time rate of change of current through the coil. Its unit is Henry (H).

## Problems

**Problem 1.** A battery of constant emf $\mathcal{E}_0$ is connected to a circuit of resistance $R$ and inductance $L$ as shown in **Figure 3**. What is the current flowing in the circuit at any time $t$?

*Hint:* The total emf (i.e. the emf of the battery plus the induced emf) in the circuit is equal to the voltage dropped across the resistance, i.e.

$$\mathcal{E}_0-L\frac{dI}{dt}=IR$$

$$\Rightarrow L\frac{dI}{dt}=\mathcal{E}_0-IR=R\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{R}-I\right)$$

$$\Rightarrow \frac{dI}{dt}=\frac{R}{L}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{R}-I\right)$$

$$\Rightarrow -\frac{d(\mathcal{E}_0/R-I)}{(\mathcal{E}_0/R-I)}=\frac{R}{L}\,dt$$

Upon integration,

$$\ln\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{R}-I\right)=-\frac{R}{L}t+C_0 \tag{9}$$

$C_0$ is the constant of integration and can be determined using the initial condition. Just before the switch is on, i.e. at $t=0$, there is no current, $I=0$, which gives $C_0=\ln(\mathcal{E}_0/R)$. Therefore, equation (9) becomes

$$\ln\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{R}-I\right)=-\frac{R}{L}t+\ln\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{R}\right)$$

$$\Rightarrow \frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{R}-I=\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{R}e^{-(R/L)t}$$

$$\therefore\ I(t)=\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{R}\left(1-e^{-(R/L)t}\right) \tag{10}$$

**Problem 2.** Find the self-inductance per unit length of a long solenoid, of radius $R$, carrying $n$ turns per unit length.

*Hint:* Consider a portion of length $l$ of the solenoid. The number of turns in the portion is $N=nl$. The magnetic field inside the solenoid is $B=\mu_0 nI$, parallel to the axis and constant. The magnetic flux linked to the portion:

$$\Phi=(\mu_0 nI)(nl)(\pi R^2)=(\mu_0\pi n^2R^2l)I$$

Comparing this with $\Phi=LI$, the self inductance is

$$L=\mu_0\pi n^2R^2l$$

and the self inductance per unit length is

$$L/l=\mu_0\pi n^2R^2$$

## Mutual Induction

We consider two coils $C_1$ and $C_2$ placed very close to each other as shown in **Figure 1**. When switch $S$ is closed, current starts rising in coil $C_1$, and hence the flux linked with it increases. This increase in flux induces a back emf in coil $C_1$ due to self induction and also causes a change in flux linked with coil $C_2$. The change in flux in coil $C_2$ induces an emf in it.

Suppose $I_1$ is the current flowing through $C_1$; then it is found that the flux linked with coil $C_2$ (i.e. $\Phi_2$) is proportional to the current $I_1$, i.e.

$$\Phi_2\propto I_1 \Rightarrow \Phi_2=M_{21}I_1$$

Here $M_{21}$ is a constant and is called the coefficient of mutual induction of coil $C_2$ with respect to coil $C_1$.

Now, from Faraday's law, the induced emf is

$$\mathcal{E}=-\frac{d\Phi_2}{dt}=-\frac{d(M_{21}I_1)}{dt}$$

$$\therefore\ \mathcal{E}=-M_{21}\frac{dI_1}{dt}$$

This gives the induced emf in coil $C_2$.

## Neumann Formula and Reciprocity Theorem

Consider two loops labeled 1 and 2 as shown in **Figure 2**. The elemental lengths $d\vec{l}_1$ on loop 1 and $d\vec{l}_2$ on loop 2 are separated by the separation vector $\vec{r}$. If a steady current $I_1$ is allowed to flow in loop 1, then it produces the magnetic field $\vec{B}_1$ around it. Some of the field lines pass through loop 2, resulting in the flux $\Phi_2$ through loop 2.

Using the Biot-Savart law, the magnetic field due to loop 1 is

$$\vec{B}_1=\frac{\mu_0 I_1}{4\pi}\oint_{\text{Loop 1}}\frac{d\vec{l}_1\times\hat{r}}{r^2}$$

and the corresponding vector potential is

$$\vec{A}_1=\frac{\mu_0 I_1}{4\pi}\oint_{\text{Loop 1}}\frac{d\vec{l}_1}{r}$$

The magnetic flux through Loop 2 due to $\vec{B}_1$ is

$$\Phi_2=\int_{S_2}\vec{B}_1\cdot d\vec{a}_2=\int_{S_2}(\nabla\times\vec{A}_1)\cdot d\vec{a}_2=\oint_{\text{Loop 2}}\vec{A}_1\cdot d\vec{l}_2$$

Substituting the expression for $\vec{A}_1$, we have

$$\Phi_2=\oint_{\text{Loop 2}}\frac{\mu_0 I_1}{4\pi}\oint_{\text{Loop 1}}\frac{d\vec{l}_1}{r}\cdot d\vec{l}_2=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint_{\text{Loop 1}}\oint_{\text{Loop 2}}\frac{d\vec{l}_1\cdot d\vec{l}_2}{r}I_1=M_{21}I_1$$

with

$$M_{21}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint_{\text{Loop 1}}\oint_{\text{Loop 2}}\frac{d\vec{l}_1\cdot d\vec{l}_2}{r} \tag{1}$$

This is known as the Neumann formula for mutual induction, and it shows that the coefficient of mutual induction depends only upon the geometrical quantities (shape, size, position) of the loops, not on the current flowing through them. Similarly, we can show

$$\Phi_1=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint_{\text{Loop 2}}\oint_{\text{Loop 1}}\frac{d\vec{l}_2\cdot d\vec{l}_1}{r}I_2=M_{12}I_2$$

with

$$M_{12}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint_{\text{Loop 2}}\oint_{\text{Loop 1}}\frac{d\vec{l}_2\cdot d\vec{l}_1}{r}$$

Since $\vec{dl}_1\cdot\vec{dl}_2=\vec{dl}_2\cdot\vec{dl}_1$, we get $M_{12}=M_{21}$. If $I_1=I_2$, then $\Phi_1=\Phi_2$; this is called the reciprocity theorem. According to this theorem:

Whatever the shapes and positions of the loops, the flux through loop 2 when a current $I$ flows around loop 1 is exactly the same as the flux through loop 1 when the same current flows around loop 2.

## Energy Stored in the Magnetic Field of a Coil

We suppose a coil is connected in series with a battery as shown in **Figure 3**. As soon as the switch $S$ is closed, the current starts rising in the circuit. The back emf produced in the coil opposes this rise in current. So, work must be done against the back emf to get the current going. At steady current, no further work is done against the back emf.

Suppose the current increases from zero to $I$, and $L$ is the self inductance of the coil; then the emf produced is

$$\mathcal{E}=-L\frac{dI}{dt}$$

and the back emf is $-\mathcal{E}=L\,dI/dt$. So, the rate of doing work against the emf, i.e. the power delivered by the battery, is

$$P=-\mathcal{E}I=LI\frac{dI}{dt}$$

Thus the total work done is

$$W=\int_0^I P\,dt=\int_0^I LI\frac{dI}{dt}\,dt=L\int_0^I I\,dI$$

$$\therefore\ W=\frac{1}{2}LI^2 \tag{2}$$

Also, the magnetic flux linked with the coil is $\Phi=LI$, and we know

$$\Phi=\int_S\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{a}=\int_S(\nabla\times\vec{A})\cdot d\vec{a}=\oint_C\vec{A}\cdot d\vec{l}$$

where $C$ is the path of integration for the loop and $S$ is the area bounded by it.

$$\therefore\ LI=\oint_C\vec{A}\cdot d\vec{l} \tag{3}$$

From equations (2) and (3),

$$W=\frac{1}{2}I\oint_C\vec{A}\cdot d\vec{l}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\text{volume}}\vec{A}\cdot\vec{J}\,d\tau \tag{4}$$

From Ampere's law,

$$\nabla\times\vec{B}=\mu_0\vec{J} \Rightarrow \vec{J}=\frac{1}{\mu_0}(\nabla\times\vec{B})$$

so equation (4) becomes

$$W=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{volume}}\vec{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{B})\,d\tau \tag{5}$$

For vectors $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ we know that

$$\nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})=\vec{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{A})-\vec{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{B})$$

$$\Rightarrow \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{B})=\vec{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{A})-\nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})$$

$$\Rightarrow \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{B})=\vec{B}\cdot\vec{B}-\nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})=B^2-\nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})$$

So, equation (5) reduces to

$$W=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{volume}}B^2\,d\tau-\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{volume}}\nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})\,d\tau$$

Applying the divergence theorem to the second term on the right-hand side,

$$W=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{volume}}B^2\,d\tau-\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\oint_{\text{surface}}(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})\cdot d\vec{a} \tag{6}$$

Far from the current loop, both $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ decrease and vanish at infinite distance. So the surface integral over the surface of infinitely large radius vanishes. The total energy over all space is due only to the volume integral part. So, for integration over all space we get

$$W=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{all space}}B^2\,d\tau \tag{7}$$

This gives the magnetic energy stored in the given volume. Thus, the energy stored per unit volume (i.e. energy density) is

$$u_m=\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} \tag{8}$$

## Problems

**Problem 1.** A short solenoid (length $l$ and radius $a$, with $n_1$ turns per unit length) lies on the axis of a very long solenoid (radius $b$, $n_2$ turns per unit length) as shown in **Figure 4**. Current $I$ flows in the short solenoid. What is the flux through the long solenoid? What is the mutual induction?

*Hint:* Even though the detailed information is given for the short solenoid, it is very difficult to find the magnetic field and hence the flux linked to it. But using the reciprocity theorem we can resolve the problem by sending the same current through the long solenoid. Then we can have $\Phi_{\text{short}}=\Phi_{\text{long}}$.

Now, the magnetic field in the long solenoid is $B=\mu_0 n_2 I$. The flux through the short solenoid due to the field of the long solenoid is

$$\Phi_{\text{short}}=(\mu_0 n_2 I)(n_1 l)(\pi a^2)=(\mu_0\pi a^2 n_1 n_2 l)I$$

Hence, the flux through the long solenoid when the same current flows through the short solenoid is

$$\Phi_{\text{long}}=\Phi_{\text{short}}=(\mu_0 n_2 I)(n_1 l)(\pi a^2)=(\mu_0\pi a^2 n_1 n_2 l)I$$

Therefore, the mutual inductance is

$$M=\mu_0\pi a^2 n_1 n_2 l$$

**Problem 2.** A long coaxial cable carries current $I$ (the current flows down the surface of the inner cylinder, radius $a$, and back along the outer cylinder, radius $b$) as shown in **Figure 5**. Find the magnetic energy stored in a section of length $l$. Also, find the coefficient of self-induction.

*Hint:* First of all we have to calculate the magnetic field due to the system of currents. For this purpose, let us take a coaxial Amperian loop (blue circle) of radius $r$ passing through point $P$ as shown in **Figure 6**. Ampere's law reads,

$$\oint\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{l}=\mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}$$

But

$$I_{\text{enc}}=\begin{cases}I-I=0, & \text{for } P \text{ outside the outer cylinder } (r>b)\\ I, & \text{for } P \text{ between the two cylinders } (a<r<b)\\ 0, & \text{for } P \text{ inside the inner cylinder } (r<a)\end{cases}$$

$$\Rightarrow B(2\pi r)=\mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}$$

$$\Rightarrow B=\frac{\mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}}{2\pi r} \tag{9}$$

Therefore, the magnetic field is

$$B=\begin{cases}0, & r>b\\ \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}, & a<r<b\\ 0, & r<a\end{cases} \tag{10}$$

To calculate the magnetostatic energy stored in the system, we are going to use

$$W_m=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{all space}}B^2\,d\tau$$

For this purpose, let us take an annular cylindrical portion of radius $r'$, thickness $dr'$, and length $l$ as shown in **Figure 6**. The elemental volume is $d\tau=2\pi r'dr'l$. Therefore, the energy stored is

$$W_m=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{all space}}B^2\,d\tau=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_0^\infty B^2(2\pi r'dr'l)=\frac{\pi l}{\mu_0}\int_0^\infty B^2 r'\,dr'$$

$$=\frac{\pi l}{\mu_0}\left[\int_0^a B^2 r'\,dr'+\int_a^b B^2 r'\,dr'+\int_b^\infty B^2 r'\,dr'\right]$$

where $B=0$ for $r'<a$ and $B=0$ for $r'>b$, so only the middle term survives:

$$=\frac{\pi l}{\mu_0}\int_a^b\left(\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r'}\right)^2 r'\,dr'=\frac{\pi l}{\mu_0}\cdot\frac{\mu_0^2 I^2}{4\pi^2}\int_a^b\frac{dr'}{r'}=\frac{\mu_0 l I^2}{4\pi}\ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$$

$$\therefore\ W_m=\frac{\mu_0 l I^2}{4\pi}\ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$$

Comparing this with $W=\frac{1}{2}LI^2$, the self-inductance is given by

$$L=\frac{\mu_0 l}{2\pi}\ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$$

**Problem 3.** Find the energy stored in a section of length $l$ of a long solenoid (radius $R$, current $I$, and $n$ turns per unit length), and hence obtain the expression for the self inductance.

*Hint:* The magnetic field inside the solenoid is $B=\mu_0 nI$, constant everywhere inside and zero outside. The volume of the solenoid is $V_{\text{sol}}=\pi R^2 l$.

The energy stored:

$$W_m=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{all space}}B^2\,d\tau=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{solenoid}}B^2\,d\tau=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2V_{\text{sol}}$$

$$=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}(\mu_0 nI)^2\pi R^2 l=\frac{1}{2}\pi\mu_0 n^2R^2lI^2$$

Comparing this with $W=\frac{1}{2}LI^2$, the self inductance is

$$L=\pi\mu_0 n^2R^2l$$
